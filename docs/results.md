# Results

What the comparison actually produces, including the cases where FleetFlow does
not win. Every number here is reproducible from a committed command; nothing is
rounded up in its favour.

## What is being compared

A run drives **one** seeded demand through **two** engines at once, stepped by one
clock, so the two arms see the same vehicles, the same departures and the same
origin-destination pairs:

- **baseline** — static free-flow weights. Its route queries never change, so the
  only thing it knows is road length and speed limit.
- **fleetflow** — dynamic weights, fed by its own vehicles' speed reports. The
  reports go over `POST /api/reports` on the public API, so simulated traffic is
  validated and measured exactly like an external client (T40a).

Both arms' fleet vehicles *do* ask for a new route periodically. In the baseline
arm that request is answered by its own static metric, so the new route equals the
road it is already on and it always declines: the `route_changes` column is 0 for
the baseline by construction, not because it does not try.

This is a simulator, not a field trial. What it can show is that the mechanism
works and under what conditions; it cannot show what a real fleet in Jakarta would
save, and the sections below say where that line is.

## The demo network

Generated by `scripts/generate_grid.go`, and the generation is committed so the
data is not a mystery:

```sh
go run ./scripts/generate_grid.go -spacing-m 150 -lanes 1
```

36 nodes, 120 directed edges, 150 m blocks, one lane per street and two on the
central arterials. Capacity per edge is `lanes * length_m / 7.5` (DESIGN 3.1), so
20 to 40 vehicles per edge and 3,200 across the graph.

**Why so small.** Capacity is set by the network, not by the fleet, and the
relationship decides whether the comparison says anything at all. On the larger
grid this project started with (450 m blocks, two and three lanes) capacity is
16,800 vehicles: a run of even 5,000 vehicles peaks near 1,000 vehicles on the
network at once, under 1% of capacity, and `EdgeSpeed` is BPR with a fourth power,
so nothing slows down. Measured mid-run at 5,000 vehicles on that grid,
`cost_s / free_flow_s` was exactly 1 on every edge, `route_changes` was 0 in both
arms, and every trip finished within 0.0007 s of its free-flow time. Both arms were
identical, and no vehicle count or reroute threshold fixes that.

**Why a 30 s window.** The other half of the same arithmetic: what congests a road
is how many trips are on it *at once*, and that is the fleet times trip duration
over the departure window. Spreading 2,000 vehicles over the 600 s default leaves
about 130 of them on the network at a time, which is a tenth of the capacity of a
single busy edge. Concentrating the same 2,000 departures into 30 s is what a
morning peak does, and it is what gives a dynamic router something to avoid. The
window matters more than the vehicle count.

## The demo scenario, ten seeds

Defaults: `seed 1`, `2000 vehicles`, `fleet_share 0.5`, `step_s 1`, `window_s 30`,
`reroute_every_s 30`, `reroute_threshold 0.10`.

go run ./scripts/compare -seed-from 1 -seed-to 10

the two arms are within a few seconds of each other.

## Across fleet shares, ten seeds each

```sh
go run ./scripts/compare -seed-from 1 -seed-to 10 -fleet-share 0
go run ./scripts/compare -seed-from 1 -seed-to 10 -fleet-share 0.25
go run ./scripts/compare -seed-from 1 -seed-to 10 -fleet-share 0.75
go run ./scripts/compare -seed-from 1 -seed-to 10 -fleet-share 1
```

| fleet share | mean Δ average trip time | mean Δ p95 | seeds better / worse / tied |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | **+0.0000 s** | +0.0000 s | 0 / 0 / **10** |
| 0.25 | −15.13 s | −131.85 s | 9 / 1 / 0 |
| 0.50 | −20.21 s | −175.78 s | 9 / 1 / 0 |
| 0.75 | −20.76 s | −180.46 s | 9 / 1 / 0 |
| 1.00 | −21.76 s | −187.49 s | **10** / 0 / 0 |

The zero row is the control, and it is the reason to trust the others: with no
vehicle using the dynamic router the two arms are identical to the digit on all ten
seeds. Everything else the two arms share — the graph, the demand, the simulation,
the clock — is therefore not producing the difference; the routing is.

The benefit rises with the share and then flattens: by 50% the fleet has already
avoided most of the jam, and doubling the remaining share buys about 1.5 s more.
At 100% it wins on every seed.

## Where FleetFlow does not win

**Seed 3, at every non-zero share below 100%** (+3.58 s at 0.25, +2.45 s at 0.5,
+2.12 s at 0.75). On this seed the baseline does not jam — its average is 49.2 s —
so there is nothing to route around, and the FleetFlow arm pays for detours that
buy nothing. It is a small loss on a large win elsewhere, but it is a loss, and it
is the honest answer to "does dynamic weighting always help": no, not when the
static route was already fine. At fleet share 1 it does not occur.

**A wide departure window.** With `-window-s 120` and everything else at the demo
defaults, the mean difference over three seeds is −0.0078 s with the sign varying
from seed to seed, and `route_changes` is 0 in both arms. That is a tie: a
hundredth of a second on a 39 s trip is float noise, not a result. See the caveat
on the tie tolerance below. The network simply does not congest at that density.

**Any configuration that does not congest.** The larger grid in "The demo network"
above is the extreme case, and it is where this investigation started: identical
arms, every figure tied, at every density the simulator can drive.

## What the dashboard shows of it

Measured through the served pipeline — a real server, a real stream client — at the
demo defaults: the FleetFlow engine's own weights peak at **`cost_s / free_flow_s` =
2.12** during the peak, so that panel does turn amber and red as its reports arrive,
and its opening frame of 4,000 vehicles drains to 166 at 400 simulated seconds. The
whole run settles in about twenty seconds of wall clock, at the server's 50 ms step.

**The baseline panel stays green while its traffic jams**, and that is the contract
rather than a bug: the colours show the weights an arm is routing on, and the
baseline's weights are fixed at free flow by definition, so it has nothing to show.
Its jam is visible elsewhere — read mid-run, the baseline arm reported 0.83% of edges
saturated with a utilization standard deviation of 0.35, while the FleetFlow arm had
already finished and reported 0 and 0. So the difference lives in the metrics strip,
in the route-change count and in how the two arms' vehicles move, not in the colour
of the baseline map. Putting that jam on the map as well would mean streaming each
arm's occupancy rather than one engine's metric; DESIGN 3.5 records why it does the
latter.

## Caveats

- **The congestion columns in a finished run are always zero.**
  `saturated_edges_pct` and `utilization_std_dev` are a snapshot of the network at
  the moment the metrics are collected, and a run that has settled has nothing left
  on its roads — so they read 0.0000 in every row above, including the rows where
  the baseline's trips are five times slower than free flow. They are meaningful
  mid-run, which is what `GET /api/metrics` and the dashboard show. This record
  does not use them.
- **The tie tolerance is tight.** The tool calls a difference a difference above
  1e-6 relative, so differences of a few thousandths of a second are reported as
  wins rather than ties. That is why the wide-window case reads "better on 2, worse
  on 1" while being a tie on any sensible reading.
- **`route_changes` counts reroutes after departure.** A FleetFlow vehicle can also
  take a different route from its departure, so the arms can differ slightly with
  this column at 0 in both.
- **The baseline is a strawman by design.** It is a fleet with no telemetry at all.
  A real baseline for a fleet operator would be their own routing, which might well
  use traffic data from elsewhere. The comparison here is "does feeding a fleet its
  own observations help", not "is FleetFlow better than the state of the art".
- **The capacity assumption drives everything.** `capacity = lanes * length_m / 7.5`
  is a modelled storage capacity, not a measured one, and the size of the network
  relative to the fleet is what decides whether any of this matters. That
  sensitivity is stated above rather than buried.

## What the record does and does not show

**Shows:** when a road network congests, a fleet that routes on its own observed
speeds avoids the jams it creates, and a fleet that routes statically does not. The
effect is monotone in the share of vehicles connected, exactly zero when none are,
and it survives ten seeds with the losses reported beside the wins.

**Does not show:** that FleetFlow would help a real fleet in Jakarta. The graph is
synthetic, demand is uniform rather than directional, capacity is an assumption,
and the baseline has no traffic information at all. The honest summary is that the
mechanism is demonstrated, not that the benefit is general.
